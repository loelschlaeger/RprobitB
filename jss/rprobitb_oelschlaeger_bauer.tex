\title{\pkg{RprobitB}: Bayesian Probit Choice Models with Preference
Heterogeneity in \proglang{R}}
\Plaintitle{RprobitB: Bayesian Probit Choice Models with Preference
Heterogeneity in R}
\Shorttitle{\pkg{RprobitB}: Bayesian Probit Choice Models in \proglang{R}}

\Abstract{
The \proglang{R} package \pkg{RprobitB} provides efficient Bayesian estimation
of multinomial probit models for discrete choice data, with a focus on
unobserved preference heterogeneity. Heterogeneity is modeled through random
coefficients with normal or log-normal mixing distributions and through latent
classes, whose number is fixed in advance or inferred from the data by sparse
finite or Dirichlet process mixtures. Estimation is based on a Gibbs sampler
with data augmentation, which avoids the costly evaluation of the probit
likelihood. Besides multinomial choices, the package supports binary, ordered,
and ranked responses in cross-sectional and panel data. Posterior inference
comprises marginal effects, willingness-to-pay measures, and predicted choice
probabilities with credible intervals. Competing specifications are compared by
information criteria, approximate leave-one-out cross-validation, and Bayes
factors. We describe the model class, the sampler, and the software design, and
illustrate the workflow with five empirical data sets.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

Throughout life, individuals make countless choices: commuters select a mode of
transport, consumers a product, and patients a treatment. Companies and
policymakers seek to understand which attributes shape such decisions and how a
modification of these attributes influences choice behavior. The analysis of
choice behavior is therefore pervasive in applied fields such as transportation
\citep{Helveston2015}, marketing \citep{Brand2022}, and health economics
\citep{vanKinschot2021}. In each of these fields, deciders may value the same
attributes differently. Some travelers pay a high fare to save time, whereas
others accept a long trip if it is cheap, and such differences often stem from
tastes and attitudes that are difficult to measure. This preference
heterogeneity is frequently of primary interest, because the response to a fare
increase depends on how price sensitivity is distributed over the travelers, and
because markets often consist of segments with distinct tastes. Empirical
research therefore requires a statistical model that infers the distribution of
preferences in a population of deciders from observed choices.

Statistical inference on choices among a discrete set of alternatives
traditionally builds upon random utility models \citep{Train2009}. The utility
of an alternative is a linear combination of covariates plus a random error, the
decider chooses the alternative with the largest utility, and only this choice
is observed. The multinomial logit model has closed-form choice probabilities
but implies the independence of irrelevant alternatives, whereas the multinomial
probit model assumes jointly normal errors, which may be correlated across
alternatives and thereby allow flexible substitution patterns. However, its
choice probabilities are multivariate normal integrals without a closed form,
which maximum likelihood estimation must simulate, for instance with the
Geweke--Hajivassiliou--Keane simulator \citep{Hajivassiliou1996}, or
approximate, for instance with the Solow--Joe approximation \citep{Solow1990,
Joe1995}.

Unobserved preference heterogeneity is commonly modeled through random
coefficients that take decider-specific values from a mixing distribution. This
approach was introduced in the probit context by \citet{Hausman1978} and became
popular with the mixed logit model \citep{McFadden2000} and with hierarchical
Bayesian models in marketing \citep{Allenby1998}. The mixing distribution must
be specified by the analyst and is frequently assumed to be normal
\citep[Chapter~6]{Train2009}, a choice that is often limited by the software
and not always checked. This assumption is violated under domain constraints,
multimodal preference distributions, or asymmetric heterogeneity
\citep{Cirillo2006, Scarpa2008, Caputo2018}. A misspecified mixing distribution
distorts derived quantities such as the value of travel time savings
\citep{Hess2005, Fosgerau2006}. Latent class models instead partition
the deciders into segments with class-specific coefficients \citep{Kamakura1989,
Greene2003, Kim2023} and require a specification of the number of classes. For
the probit model, random coefficients increase the computational burden further,
because they induce dependencies across a decider's choice occasions and
multiply the dimension of the likelihood integrals.

The \proglang{R} \citep{RCoreTeam2026} package \pkg{RprobitB}\footnote{The
package name is a portmanteau of the language \proglang{R}, the probit model,
and the Bayes framework.} \citep{Oelschlaeger2026d} approaches these challenges
through Bayesian estimation, which avoids the evaluation of the likelihood
integrals, admits flexible mixing distributions, and can infer the number of
classes from the data. Following \citet{Albert1993}, \citet{McCulloch1994}, and
\citet{Imai2005a}, a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler treats the latent
utilities as additional parameters and draws them together with the model
parameters. Conditional on the utilities, the probit model is a linear
regression with normal errors, in which the coefficients have normal and the
covariance matrices have inverse Wishart full conditional distributions. The
decider-specific coefficients and the class allocations are also drawn from
their full conditional distributions. Random coefficients follow normal or
log-normal mixing distributions, the latter for coefficients whose sign is fixed
by theory, and may be correlated. Combined with latent classes, they yield the
latent class mixed probit model of \citet{Oelschlaeger2021}, whose mixture of
normals approximates skewed or multimodal preference distributions and
classifies the deciders by their preferences \citep{Oelschlaeger2026c}. If the
number of classes is not fixed in advance, a sparse finite mixture starts with
more classes than needed, and a prior that favors small class weights empties
the superfluous classes during sampling
\citep{Rousseau2011, MalsinerWalli2016, FruehwirthSchnatter2019}. Alternatively,
a Dirichlet process mixture treats the number of occupied classes as random
\citep{Escobar1995, Neal2000}, and the weight-based updating scheme of
\citet{Oelschlaeger2021} removes, splits, and merges classes according to their
weights and means. From the posterior draws, the package derives the population
distribution of preferences, class membership probabilities, decider-specific
coefficients, willingness-to-pay measures, marginal effects, and predictions
conditional on the choice history of a decider, together with their posterior
uncertainty.

The package also supports ordered and ranked responses, cross-sectional and
panel data, and individual choice sets. All models are fitted with a single
function \fct{fit}, which returns an S3 object that supports standard methods
such as \fct{summary}, \fct{plot}, \fct{coef}, \fct{predict}, and \fct{update}.
Several Gibbs chains can run in parallel through the \pkg{future} framework
\citep{Bengtsson2021}. The draws are stored in the format of the \pkg{posterior}
package \citep{Buerkner2026}, which provides the convergence diagnostics of
\citet{Vehtari2021}, and are displayed with \pkg{bayesplot} \citep{Gabry2026}.
Choice data handling and simulation come from the companion package
\pkg{choicedata} \citep{Oelschlaeger2026a}. Competing specifications are
compared by information criteria and approximate leave-one-out cross-validation
with the \pkg{loo} package \citep{Vehtari2017, Vehtari2026}, and by Bayes
factors estimated by bridge sampling \citep{Meng1996, Gronau2020}.

Existing open-source software covers the combination of probit models and
preference heterogeneity only in part, as Table~\ref{tab:software} summarizes.
The \proglang{R} packages \pkg{mlogit} \citep{Croissant2020}, \pkg{gmnl}
\citep{Sarrias2017}, \pkg{logitr} \citep{Helveston2023}, and \pkg{apollo}
\citep{Hess2019}, as well as \pkg{Biogeme} \citep{Bierlaire2020, Bierlaire2025}
for \proglang{Python} \citep{VanRossum2009}, model heterogeneity in logit models
with a mixing distribution or a number of classes that the analyst fixes in
advance, by maximum (simulated) likelihood or, in the case of \pkg{apollo} and
\pkg{Biogeme}, also by Bayesian estimation. \pkg{bayesm} \citep{Rossi2025}
relaxes this restriction through finite and Dirichlet process mixtures of
normals, but only for the logit model. For the probit model, in contrast, the
multinomial models of \pkg{mlogit}, \pkg{bayesm}, and \pkg{MNP}
\citep{Imai2005b, Imai2026} have fixed coefficients, and \pkg{apollo},
\pkg{Biogeme}, and \pkg{Rchoice} \citep{Sarrias2016} offer random coefficients
for binary and ordered responses only. The frequentist counterpart of
\pkg{RprobitB} is \pkg{Rprobit} \citep{Bauer2024}. It estimates multinomial
probit models with normal mixing distributions by maximum likelihood or by the
maximum approximate composite marginal likelihood approach of \citet{Bhat2011},
with analytic approximations of the normal probabilities such as the Solow--Joe
approximation. To the best of our knowledge, \pkg{RprobitB} is the only package
that estimates multinomial probit models with random coefficients and correlated
errors in a Bayesian framework, and the only one that infers the number of
latent classes of such a model from the data. The package is available from the
Comprehensive \proglang{R} Archive Network (CRAN) at
\url{https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=RprobitB}, and its development version
is hosted at \url{https://github.com/loelschlaeger/RprobitB}.

\section{The probit model} \label{sec:model}

Assume that we observe the choices of $N$ deciders, where decider
$n = 1, \dots, N$ faces $T_n \geq 1$ choice occasions. At occasion
$t = 1, \dots, T_n$, the decider chooses among a finite set of $J \geq 2$
mutually exclusive and exhaustive alternatives \citep[Chapter~2]{Train2009}. The
case $T_n = 1$ for all $n$ corresponds to a cross section, and $T_n > 1$ to a
panel data set, which may be unbalanced. With $J = 2$, the model is the binary
probit model.

\subsection{Utilities and choices} \label{sec:model:utility}

Every alternative $j = 1, \dots, J$ provides decider $n$ at occasion $t$ with a
latent utility
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:utility}
  U_{ntj} = X_{ntj}^\top \theta_n + \varepsilon_{ntj},
\end{equation}
%
where $X_{ntj} \in \mathbb{R}^P$ collects the covariates and $\theta_n \in
\mathbb{R}^P$ the coefficients of decider $n$. The error vector
$\varepsilon_{nt} = (\varepsilon_{nt1}, \dots, \varepsilon_{ntJ})^\top \sim
\mathrm{N}_J(0, \Sigma)$ is multivariate normal with a $J \times J$ covariance
matrix $\Sigma$ and independent across deciders and occasions. The decider
chooses the available alternative with the largest utility,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:link}
  y_{nt} = \operatorname*{argmax}_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{nt}} U_{ntj},
\end{equation}
%
where $y_{nt} = j$ denotes the event that decider $n$ chooses alternative $j$ at
occasion $t$, and $\mathcal{J}_{nt} \subseteq \{1, \dots, J\}$ is the set of
alternatives that are available at this occasion.

\subsection{Covariate types and the model formula} \label{sec:model:formula}

Covariates differ in whether their values and their coefficients vary across
alternatives. The price of a product is alternative-specific and usually has the
same coefficient in every utility. The income of a buyer is constant across
alternatives and can only enter through alternative-specific coefficients. Like
the price, the travel time varies across alternatives, but its coefficient may
differ between a car, which the traveler drives, and a train, in which the
traveler can read or work. To distinguish these three types, we write
(\ref{eq:utility}) as
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:utility_gen}
  U_{ntj} = \theta_{j}^{0} + A_{ntj}^\top \theta^{A} + B_{nt}^\top
  \theta_{j}^{B} + C_{ntj}^\top \theta_{j}^{C} + \varepsilon_{ntj},
\end{equation}
%
where the decider index of the coefficients is suppressed. The covariates
$A_{ntj}$ and $C_{ntj}$ depend on the alternative, while $B_{nt}$ depends on the
decider and the occasion only. The coefficient $\theta^{A}$ is the same for all
alternatives, whereas $\theta_{j}^{B}$ and $\theta_{j}^{C}$ are
alternative-specific. The intercepts $\theta_{j}^{0}$ are the
alternative-specific constants (ASCs). \pkg{RprobitB} specifies
(\ref{eq:utility_gen}) through a \class{formula} object of the form
\code{choice ~ A | B | C}, where \code{choice} is the response variable and
\code{A}, \code{B}, and \code{C} are the covariates of the three types. For
example, the formula \code{choice ~ price | income | time} gives the price one
coefficient for all alternatives, and the income and the travel time one
coefficient per alternative. The notation follows \pkg{mlogit} and builds on the
multi-part formulas of the \pkg{Formula} package \citep{Zeileis2010}. ASCs are
included by default and removed by \code{0} in the second part, as in
\code{choice ~ A | 0 | C}. We call every coefficient of
(\ref{eq:utility_gen}) an effect. The package labels every effect by its
covariate and, if the coefficient is alternative-specific, by the alternative.
For the formula above and the alternatives bus, car, and train, the labels
include \code{price}, \code{income_car}, \code{time_bus}, and \code{ASC_car}.

\subsection{Normalization} \label{sec:model:normalization}

Utilities are identified only up to level and scale. Adding the same constant to
all $U_{ntj}$ or multiplying all of them by a positive number leaves the choice
behavior implied by (\ref{eq:link}) unchanged
\citep[Chapter~2]{Train2009}. We normalize the level by taking differences with
respect to a base alternative $k$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:utility_diff}
  \tilde{U}_{ntj} = \tilde{X}_{ntj}^\top \theta_n + \tilde{\varepsilon}_{ntj},
  \qquad j \neq k,
\end{equation}
%
with $\tilde{U}_{ntj} = U_{ntj} - U_{ntk}$, $\tilde{X}_{ntj} = X_{ntj} -
X_{ntk}$, and $\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ntj} = \varepsilon_{ntj} -
\varepsilon_{ntk}$. The vector $\tilde{\varepsilon}_{nt}$ of the $J - 1$ error
differences is multivariate normal with mean zero and a $(J - 1) \times (J - 1)$
covariance matrix $\tilde{\Sigma}$. Differencing also shows that only the
differences of the ASCs and of the type \code{B} coefficients from those of the
base are identified. The base coefficients $\theta_{k}^{0}$ and $\theta_{k}^{B}$
are hence fixed at zero, and the remaining ones are interpreted relative to the
base, which is the first alternative in alphabetical order unless the argument
\code{base} of \fct{fit} names another one.

The scale is normalized by fixing one parameter, by default the error variance
of the first utility difference, $\tilde{\Sigma}_{11} = 1$. The argument
\code{scale} of \fct{fit} selects a different normalization, for example
\code{scale = c(price = -1)}, which fixes a coefficient instead of a variance
and measures every other coefficient in units of the fixed one. With a price
coefficient of $-1$, every other coefficient has a direct willingness-to-pay
interpretation, as in the electricity example of
Section~\ref{sec:random_coefficients}.

\subsection{Random coefficients} \label{sec:model:random}

Preferences can differ between deciders \citep{BenAkiva1993}, and the repeated
choices of a decider are informative about the coefficients of this decider
\citep{Allenby1998}. \pkg{RprobitB} therefore splits the coefficient vector into
$P_f$ fixed effects $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^{P_f}$ that are common to all
deciders and $P_r$ random effects with a decider-specific value. The random
effects are functions of a latent vector $\beta_n \in \mathbb{R}^{P_r}$ that is
drawn independently for every decider from a multivariate normal distribution,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:random}
  \beta_n \sim \mathrm{N}_{P_r}(b, \Omega), \qquad \theta_n = \big(\alpha^\top,
  g(\beta_n)^\top\big)^\top,
\end{equation}
%
independently of the covariates and the errors. The mean vector $b$ and the
covariance matrix $\Omega$ of this distribution are parameters of the model. The
map $g$ acts componentwise and determines the mixing distribution of each effect
$p = 1, \dots, P_r$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mixing_map}
  g_p(\beta_{np}) =
  \begin{cases}
    \beta_{np} & \text{for a normal effect,} \\
    \exp(\beta_{np}) & \text{for a log-normal effect,} \\
    -\exp(\beta_{np}) & \text{for a negative log-normal effect.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
%
A normal effect admits coefficients of either sign, distributed as
$\mathrm{N}(b_p, \Omega_{pp})$ over the population. A log-normal effect
restricts the coefficient to positive values, with median $\exp(b_p)$ and mean
$\exp(b_p + \Omega_{pp} / 2)$ over the population. A negative log-normal effect
is the negative of a log-normal variable and is appropriate when theory implies
a negative sign, for example of a price coefficient. A correlated effect has
free covariances in $\Omega$ with the other correlated effects, whereas an
uncorrelated effect has zeros in its row and column of $\Omega$ apart from the
variance on the diagonal. Table~\ref{tab:mixing} lists the six resulting mixing
distributions with the codes by which the argument \code{random_effects} of
\fct{fit} requests them, as in
\code{random_effects = c(price = "cln-", time = "cn")}. For log-normal effects,
$b$, $\Omega$, and the individual coefficients $\beta_n$ are reported on the
latent normal scale of (\ref{eq:random}).

\subsection{Latent classes} \label{sec:model:classes}

A single normal distribution is a restrictive assumption about how preferences
are distributed over a population \citep[Section~6.2]{Train2009}. \pkg{RprobitB}
follows \citet{Oelschlaeger2021} and replaces the normal distribution in
(\ref{eq:random}) by a mixture of $C$ normal distributions,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mixture}
  \beta_n \sim \sum_{c = 1}^{C} s_c\, \mathrm{N}_{P_r}(b_c, \Omega_c),
\end{equation}
%
with class weights $s = (s_1, \dots, s_C)$ satisfying $s_c > 0$ and
$\sum_c s_c = 1$, and class-specific means $b_c$ and covariances $\Omega_c$. The
mixture is equivalent to a latent class model. An allocation variable
$z_n \in \{1, \dots, C\}$ assigns decider $n$ to class $c$ with probability
$\Prob(z_n = c) = s_c$, and conditional on the allocation, $\beta_n \sim
\mathrm{N}_{P_r}(b_{z_n}, \Omega_{z_n})$. Because the allocations are parameters
with a posterior distribution, the model classifies deciders by their
preferences \citep{Oelschlaeger2026c}. The argument \code{latent_class_effects}
of \fct{fit} names the effects that differ between the latent classes. A random
effect named there follows (\ref{eq:mixture}), whereas a random effect
not named there keeps one mean and one covariance for all classes and is
uncorrelated with the effects named there. A nonrandom
effect named there takes one value per class and does not vary within a class.
With the $P_l$ effects of the latter kind collected in
$\lambda_c \in \mathbb{R}^{P_l}$, the complete coefficient vector of decider $n$
is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:partition}
  \theta_n = \big(\alpha^\top, \lambda_{z_n}^\top, g(\beta_n)^\top\big)^\top,
  \qquad P = P_f + P_l + P_r.
\end{equation}
%
If $P_l > 0$ but $P_r = 0$, (\ref{eq:partition}) is the classical
latent class model, in which all heterogeneity is due to the class membership
\citep{Kamakura1989, Greene2003}. If instead $P_r > 0$, it is the latent class
mixed probit model of \citet{Oelschlaeger2021}. The number of classes is
either fixed by the argument \code{classes} of \fct{fit}, or the number of
occupied classes is inferred from the data. The argument \code{class_update}
selects between a finite mixture whose classes all stay occupied
(\code{"fixed"}), an overfitted finite mixture with a sparse prior on the
weights that empties superfluous classes (\code{"sparse"};
\citealp{Rousseau2011, MalsinerWalli2016, FruehwirthSchnatter2019}), a Dirichlet
process mixture whose number of occupied classes is random
(\code{"dirichlet_process"}; \citealp{Escobar1995, Neal2000}), and the
weight-based remove, split, and merge heuristic of \citet{Oelschlaeger2021}
(\code{"weight_based"}). Section~\ref{sec:estimation} describes the
corresponding sampler steps, and Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity} applies all
four updating schemes.

\subsection{Ordered and ranked responses} \label{sec:model:ordered_ranked}

Some responses are ordered levels, such as the agreement with a statement on a
Likert scale, for which one utility per level is not natural
\citep[Section~7.4]{Train2009}. Instead, the model has a single utility
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ordered_utility}
  U_{nt} = \theta_n^0 + X_{nt}^\top \theta_n + \varepsilon_{nt}, \qquad
  \varepsilon_{nt} \sim \mathrm{N}(0, \sigma^2).
\end{equation}
%
The utility falls into one of $J$ intervals that are linked to the ordered
levels $j = 1, \dots, J$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ordered_link}
  y_{nt} = j \quad \text{if and only if} \quad \gamma_{j - 1} < U_{nt} \leq
  \gamma_{j},
\end{equation}
%
with thresholds $\gamma_0 = -\infty < \gamma_1 < \dots < \gamma_{J - 1} <
\gamma_J = +\infty$. The thresholds are parameterized through logarithmic
increments $d \in \mathbb{R}^{J - 2}$ with $\gamma_j = \sum_{i = 1}^{j - 1}
\exp(d_i)$ for $j = 2, \dots, J - 1$. The level is normalized by $\gamma_1 =
0$ and the scale by $\sigma^2 = 1$. Random coefficients and latent classes apply
to (\ref{eq:ordered_utility}) exactly as to (\ref{eq:utility}).

Some surveys record a complete ranking of the alternatives, which is more
informative about the preferences \citep[Section~7.3]{Train2009}, as we
demonstrate in Section~\ref{sec:ranked}. The utilities of
(\ref{eq:utility}) stay the same, and only the link changes. The response
at occasion $t$ is a permutation $r_{nt} = (r_{nt1}, \dots, r_{ntJ})$ of the
alternatives, ordered from best to worst, and the model requires
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ranked_link}
  U_{nt r_{nt1}} > U_{nt r_{nt2}} > \dots > U_{nt r_{ntJ}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Choice probabilities and likelihood} \label{sec:model:probabilities}

Conditional on $\theta_n$ and $\tilde{\Sigma}$, the utility differences of an
unordered choice are distributed as $\mathrm{N}_{J-1}(\tilde{X}_{nt} \theta_n,
\tilde{\Sigma})$, and the choice rule of (\ref{eq:link}) yields the
choice probability
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:choice_probability}
  \Prob(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n, \tilde{\Sigma}) =
  \begin{cases}
    \Prob\big(\tilde{U}_{ntj} > 0,\ \tilde{U}_{ntj} > \tilde{U}_{ntl} \
    \text{for all } l \notin \{j, k\}\big) & \text{if } j \neq k, \\
    \Prob\big(\tilde{U}_{ntl} < 0 \ \text{for all } l \neq k\big) & \text{if } j
    = k,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
%
where $k$ is the base alternative of Section~\ref{sec:model:normalization}.
With individual choice sets, the inequalities run over the available
alternatives only. For a ranked
response, the probability of the ranking $r_{nt}$ is the probability that all $J
- 1$ utility differences between consecutively ranked alternatives in
(\ref{eq:ranked_link}) are positive. For an ordered response, the
probability of level $j$ follows from (\ref{eq:ordered_link}),
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ordered_probability}
  \Prob(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n, \gamma, \sigma^2) =
    \Phi\Big(\frac{\gamma_j - \theta_n^0 - X_{nt}^\top \theta_n}{\sigma}\Big) -
  \Phi\Big(\frac{\gamma_{j - 1} - \theta_n^0 - X_{nt}^\top
  \theta_n}{\sigma}\Big),
\end{equation}
%
with the standard normal distribution function $\Phi$.

The population parameters of a model are those without a decider index: the
fixed coefficients $\alpha$, the class coefficients $\lambda_c$, the means $b_c$
and covariances $\Omega_c$ of the random coefficients, the class weights $s$,
the error covariance $\tilde{\Sigma}$ or the error variance $\sigma^2$, and the
thresholds $\gamma$. We collect them in the vector $\vartheta$. The random
coefficients $\beta_n$ and the allocations $z_n$ are decider-level parameters,
and the likelihood contribution of a decider integrates them out. Let
$\Prob(y_{nt} \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n, \tilde{\Sigma})$ denote the probability of
the observed response $y_{nt}$, which is (\ref{eq:choice_probability}),
its ranked analog, or (\ref{eq:ordered_probability}) with $\gamma$ and
$\sigma^2$ in place of $\tilde{\Sigma}$. With $y_n = (y_{n1},
\dots, y_{nT_n})$ and $X_n$ the responses and covariates of decider $n$, the
likelihood of the decider is
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:likelihood}
  p(y_n \mid X_n, \vartheta) = \sum_{c = 1}^{C} s_c \int \prod_{t = 1}^{T_n}
  \Prob\big(y_{nt} \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n, \tilde{\Sigma}\big)\,
  \phi(\beta_n \mid b_c, \Omega_c)\, \mathrm{d}\beta_n,
\end{equation}
%
where $\theta_n = (\alpha^\top, \lambda_c^\top, g(\beta_n)^\top)^\top$ is the
coefficient vector of (\ref{eq:partition}) for a member of class $c$, and
$\phi(\cdot \mid b_c, \Omega_c)$ is the density of $\mathrm{N}_{P_r}(b_c,
\Omega_c)$. The likelihood of the sample $y = (y_1, \dots, y_N)$ is the product
of (\ref{eq:likelihood}) over all deciders, since the deciders are
assumed to be independent given the parameters.

\section[Bayesian estimation with fit()]{Bayesian estimation with \fct{fit}}
\label{sec:estimation}

\pkg{RprobitB} estimates every model of Section~\ref{sec:model} with a Gibbs
sampler. The sampler builds on \citet{McCulloch1994} and \citet{Imai2005a} for
the multinomial probit model, on \citet{Albert1993} for the ordered probit
model, and on \citet{Oelschlaeger2021} for the latent class mixed probit model.
It relies on data augmentation \citep{Albert1993}, which treats the latent
utilities as additional parameters. Conditional on the parameters, the utilities
follow truncated normal distributions whose truncation points are determined by
the observed responses. The sampler is written in \proglang{C++} for fast
computation and linked to \proglang{R} through \pkg{Rcpp}
\citep{Eddelbuettel2011} and \pkg{RcppArmadillo} \citep{Eddelbuettel2014}. In
this section, we introduce \fct{fit}, describe the prior distributions and the
sampler, and fit models to simulated data and to the choices of train travelers
in a stated choice experiment.

\subsection[The function fit()]{The function \fct{fit}} \label{sec:fit_data}

The function \fct{fit} takes a formula and a data frame, runs the Gibbs sampler,
and returns an object of class \class{RprobitB\_fit}, on which the methods
presented in the following sections operate.
Table~\ref{tab:fit_arguments} groups its arguments by their purpose. The help
page of \fct{fit}, accessible via \code{help(fit, package = "RprobitB")},
documents each of them.

\fct{fit} accepts a \class{data.frame} in the wide or in the long layout,
selected by the argument \code{format}. In the wide layout, every row is one
choice occasion, and a covariate that varies across alternatives occupies one
column per alternative, named by the covariate, the \code{delimiter}, and the
alternative, such as \code{price_bus} and \code{price_car}. In the long layout,
every row relates to one alternative of one choice occasion, and the argument
\code{column_alternative} names the column that identifies the alternative. An
occasion may then comprise only the rows of its available alternatives, which
specifies the individual choice sets of (\ref{eq:link}). The arguments
\code{column_decider} and \code{column_occasion} name the columns that identify
the decider and, in panel data, also the choice occasion.

By default, the sampler runs four chains of 1000 iterations each, half of them
warmup. With \code{data = NULL}, \fct{fit} first simulates a data set from the
specified model and then fits it (Section~\ref{sec:fit_simulation}). The
returned object holds, among other components, the data as a
\class{choice\_data} object of \pkg{choicedata}, the hyperparameters that were
used, and the retained draws of all chains as a \class{draws\_array} object of
\pkg{posterior}.

\subsection{Prior distributions} \label{sec:est_prior}

The priors are weakly informative by default and conjugate, which gives the
normal, inverse Wishart, and Dirichlet full conditional distributions of
Section~\ref{sec:est_gibbs}. Table~\ref{tab:est_prior} lists them with their
default hyperparameters and the names under which the argument \code{prior} of
\fct{fit} accepts replacements. The coefficients $\alpha$ and $\lambda_c$ and
the class means $b_c$ have normal priors centered at zero with variance 10. The
covariance matrices $\Omega_c$ of the random coefficients and
$\tilde{\Sigma}^\ast$ of the errors have inverse Wishart priors with $\nu$ and
$\kappa$ degrees of freedom and scale matrices $\Theta$ and $\Lambda$. As in
\citet{Imai2005a}, the prior of the error covariance is placed on the matrix
$\tilde{\Sigma}^\ast$ before the scale normalization, with $\tilde{\Sigma} =
\tilde{\Sigma}^\ast / \tilde{\Sigma}^\ast_{11}$ under the default normalization.
The priors of $\lambda_c$, $b_c$, and $\Omega_c$ are independent over the
classes. The class weights $s$ of a finite mixture have a symmetric Dirichlet
prior with concentration parameter $\delta$, which by default is fixed at one
for \code{class_update = "fixed"} and \code{"weight_based"}. For the sparse
finite mixture, $\delta$ receives a gamma hyperprior with shape $a_\delta$ and
rate $b_\delta$, by default $(a_\delta, b_\delta) = (1, 200)$ as in
\citet{FruehwirthSchnatter2019}. Under the Dirichlet process, $\delta$ is the
concentration parameter of the process, with the hyperprior $(a_\delta,
b_\delta) = (2, 4)$ of \citet{Escobar1995}. The logarithmic threshold increments
$d$ of an ordered model have a standard normal prior.

\subsection{The Gibbs sampler} \label{sec:est_gibbs}

For unordered and ranked responses, the latent variables that augment the
data are the utility differences $\tilde{U}_{nt} \in \mathbb{R}^{J-1}$ of
(\ref{eq:utility_diff}). Their systematic part is
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:est_systematic}
\mu_{nt} = \tilde{W}_{nt}\alpha + \tilde{L}_{nt}\lambda_{z_n} +
\tilde{V}_{nt}\,g(\beta_n),
\end{equation}
%
where $\tilde{W}_{nt}$, $\tilde{L}_{nt}$, and $\tilde{V}_{nt}$ are the columns
of the differenced covariates that belong to $\alpha$, $\lambda_c$, and
$\beta_n$, and the error of $\tilde{U}_{nt}$ is $\tilde{\varepsilon}_{nt} \sim
\mathrm{N}_{J-1}(0, \tilde{\Sigma})$. Given the utilities and $\tilde{\Sigma}$,
the model is a linear regression. Every coefficient block
$\rho \in \mathbb{R}^{P_\rho}$ with prior $\mathrm{N}_{P_\rho}(\rho_0, V_0)$ and
design matrices $Z_{nt}$ hence has the normal full conditional distribution
%
\begin{align}
\rho \mid \cdot &\sim \mathrm{N}_{P_\rho}\big(\Sigma_\rho\, \mu_\rho,
\Sigma_\rho\big), \label{eq:est_regression} \\
\mu_\rho &= V_0^{-1}\rho_0 + \sum_{n, t} Z_{nt}^\top \tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}
(\tilde{U}_{nt} - q_{nt}), \qquad
\Sigma_\rho = \Big(V_0^{-1} + \sum_{n, t} Z_{nt}^\top \tilde{\Sigma}^{-1}
Z_{nt}\Big)^{-1}, \nonumber
\end{align}
%
where $q_{nt}$ is the part of $\mu_{nt}$ that does not depend on $\rho$. Every
chain starts at a draw from the prior distributions of the coefficients and
thresholds, with the random coefficients at their class means, $\Omega_c$ at the
prior scale $\Theta$, $\tilde{\Sigma}$ at the identity, the deciders
allocated to the classes at random, and the utilities at zero, so that the
chains start dispersed. One iteration then updates the following blocks in
turn:
%
\begin{enumerate}
\item The class weights $s$ of a finite mixture are drawn from
$\mathrm{Dir}_C(\delta + m_1, \dots, \delta + m_C)$, where $m_c$ is the number
of deciders in class $c$. Under the Dirichlet process, the weights are
integrated out by the algorithm of \citet{Neal2000}, and the package reports the
class shares $m_c / N$.
\item For every class $c$, the mean $b_c$ and the covariance $\Omega_c$ follow
the conjugate normal and inverse Wishart updates given the sample $\{\beta_n :
z_n = c\}$.
\item The class coefficients $\lambda_c$ follow
(\ref{eq:est_regression}) with $Z_{nt} = \tilde{L}_{nt}$ and the sums
over the deciders of class $c$.
\item For every decider $n$, the random coefficients $\beta_n$ follow
(\ref{eq:est_regression}) with $Z_{nt} = \tilde{V}_{nt}$, the sums over
the occasions of decider $n$, and the class distribution
$\mathrm{N}_{P_r}(b_{z_n}, \Omega_{z_n})$ as the prior. With a log-normal random
effect, an elliptical slice sampling step \citep{Murray2010} replaces this
update. The fixed coefficients $\alpha$ then follow (\ref{eq:est_regression})
with $Z_{nt} = \tilde{W}_{nt}$ and the sums over all deciders.
\item Decider $n$ is allocated to class $c$ with probability
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:est_allocation}
\Prob(z_n = c \mid \cdot) \propto s_c\, \phi(\beta_n \mid b_c, \Omega_c)
\prod_{t = 1}^{T_n} \Prob(y_{nt} \mid X_{nt}, \alpha, \lambda_c, \beta_n,
\tilde{\Sigma}).
\end{equation}
%
The four values of \code{class_update} differ in the treatment of the number of
classes. For \code{"fixed"}, the last member of a class stays in it, which keeps
all $C$ classes occupied. For \code{"sparse"}, $C$ is an upper bound, and the
small concentration parameter empties the classes that the data do not require.
For \code{"dirichlet_process"}, deciders open new classes, up to
\code{max_classes}, by Algorithm~8 of \citet{Neal2000}. For
\code{"weight_based"}, the heuristic of \citet{Oelschlaeger2021} removes,
splits, or merges classes during the warmup.
\item In an ordered model, the thresholds $\gamma$ are updated by a random-walk
Metropolis--Hastings step on their logarithmic increments $d$.
\item Given the parameters, the utility differences $\tilde{U}_{nt}$ of an
unordered choice are $\mathrm{N}_{J-1}(\mu_{nt}, \tilde{\Sigma})$ truncated to
the region in which the chosen alternative has the largest utility, and the
truncated normal draws use the samplers of \citet{Robert1995}. For a ranked
response, the truncation is chosen such that the observed ranking holds. An
ordered response has a single utility $U_{nt} = \mu_{nt} + \varepsilon_{nt}$
with $\varepsilon_{nt} \sim \mathrm{N}(0, 1)$, drawn from
$\mathrm{N}(\mu_{nt}, 1)$ truncated to $(\gamma_{y_{nt} - 1}, \gamma_{y_{nt}}]$.
\item The error covariance is drawn from $\tilde{\Sigma}^\ast \sim
\mathrm{W}^{-1}_{J-1}(\kappa + \sum_n T_n, \Lambda + S)$ with the scatter matrix
$S$ of the residuals $\tilde{U}_{nt} - \mu_{nt}$ and normalized to
$\tilde{\Sigma} = \tilde{\Sigma}^\ast / \tilde{\Sigma}^\ast_{11}$.
\end{enumerate}

Of the \code{iterations} of every chain, the first \code{warmup} are discarded
and every \code{thin}-th of the remaining ones is retained. The $D$ retained
draws of all chains are rescaled to the normalization requested by \code{scale}.
In a mixture model, a chain may swap the class labels during a run
\citep{Stephens2000}. The draws are therefore relabeled after sampling by
matching them to a reference partition \citep{Dahl2006, Papastamoulis2010}, and
the classes are numbered by decreasing posterior mean weight.

\subsection{Compensations} \label{sec:compensations}

The values of the coefficients depend on the scale normalization of
Section~\ref{sec:model:normalization}, whereas their ratios are invariant to it.
Because the utility is linear in the covariates, an increase of one unit in the
covariate of effect $p$ leaves the utility unchanged if the covariate of effect
$q$ changes by
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:compensation}
\tau_{pq} = -\frac{\theta_p}{\theta_q}
\end{equation}
%
units \citep{Train2009}, the compensation of effect $p$ in units of effect $q$.
With the price as reference effect $q$, $\tau_{pq}$ is the willingness to pay
for one unit of effect $p$: the price increase that leaves the utility of the
alternative unchanged, which is negative for an undesired effect.
\fct{interpret} evaluates (\ref{eq:compensation}) under every draw and
summarizes the posterior of each ratio. For \code{model\_train}, the
compensations with the price as reference are in euro at 1987 prices:
%
\begin{Schunk}
\begin{Sinput}
R> train_compensation <- interpret(model_train, reference = "price")
R> train_compensation
\end{Sinput}
\begin{Soutput}
1 `time` compensates -5.38 `price` (95% interval -6.26 to -4.53)
1 `change` compensates -1.02 `price` (95% interval -1.36 to -0.681)
1 `factor(comfort)1` compensates -2.04 `price` (95% interval -2.45 to -1.62)
1 `factor(comfort)2` compensates -6.84 `price` (95% interval -7.61 to -6.06)
\end{Soutput}
\end{Schunk}
%
A traveler is willing to pay about 5.4 euro to save one hour of travel time
(95\% credible interval from 4.5 to 6.3 euro), about 1.0 euro to avoid a change
of trains, and about 6.8 euro for the best instead of the worst comfort class.

\subsection{Marginal effects}

The choice probabilities depend on the utilities through the nonlinear probit
link. The partial derivative of a probability with respect to a covariate, its
marginal effect, therefore varies with the covariate values. \fct{interpret}
computes marginal effects by numerical differentiation. Let $\pi_j(X_{nt},
\vartheta) = \Prob(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \vartheta)$ denote the probability of
alternative $j$ at occasion $t$ of decider $n$, and let $X_{nt}^{+h}$ and
$X_{nt}^{-h}$ be $X_{nt}$ with a numeric covariate $x$ of alternative $j$
shifted by $+h$ and $-h$. The marginal effect of $x$ on $\pi_j$ under draw $d$
is the central difference
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal_effect}
\mathrm{ME}_{ntj}^{(d)}(x) = \frac{\pi_j\big(X_{nt}^{+h}, \vartheta^{(d)}\big) -
\pi_j\big(X_{nt}^{-h}, \vartheta^{(d)}\big)}{2h},
\end{equation}
%
where $h$ is a small step size. The marginal
effect at the average (MEA, \code{type = "mea"}) evaluates
(\ref{eq:marginal_effect}) at one artificial occasion whose covariates
are the averages or, for factors, the modes of the observed ones, and the
average marginal effect (AME, \code{type = "ame"}) averages it over all observed
choice occasions:
%

\section{Modeling preference heterogeneity} \label{sec:heterogeneity}

We let the coefficients vary between deciders through random coefficients and
latent classes. Both benefit from panel data, whose repeated choices separate
this variation from the errors.

\subsection{Random coefficients} \label{sec:random_coefficients}

\subsection{Individual deciders} \label{sec:individual}

A mixed model fitted with \code{save_individual_draws = TRUE} retains the
posterior draws of every decider's coefficients. Conditional on the population
parameters, the posterior of the random coefficient vector $\beta_n$ of decider
$n$ combines the population distribution of (\ref{eq:random}) with the
decider's own choices,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:individual_posterior}
p(\beta_n \mid y, \vartheta) \propto \phi(\beta_n \mid b, \Omega) \prod_{t =
1}^{T_n} \Prob(y_{nt} \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n, \tilde{\Sigma}).
\end{equation}
%
Equation~\ref{eq:individual_posterior} is the conditional distribution of the
coefficients given the decider's choices \citep[Chapter~11]{Train2009}, in
contrast to the unconditional population distribution of
(\ref{eq:random}). \code{coef(level = "individual")} returns the
posterior means of the individual coefficients, one row per household:
%

\subsection{Ordered responses} \label{sec:ordered}

We model the self-reported health satisfaction of adults as a function of age,
income, the degree of handicap in percent (\code{handper}), and marital status,
and quantify its persistent person-specific component. The \code{Health} data of
the \pkg{Rchoice} package, an extract of the German Socio-Economic Panel
compiled by \citet{Riphahn2003}, comprise 27326 observations of 7293 persons

\section{Prediction and residuals} \label{sec:prediction}

The \fct{predict} method computes posterior predictive choice probabilities,
whose deviations from the observed choices define the residuals of
Section~\ref{sec:residuals}. The population choice probability integrates the
choice probability of Section~\ref{sec:model:probabilities} over the population
distribution of $\theta_n$,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:population_probability}
\Prob(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \vartheta)
= \int \Prob(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n, \tilde{\Sigma})\,
  p(\theta_n \mid \vartheta)\, \mathrm{d}\theta_n,
\end{equation}
%
where $p(\theta_n \mid \vartheta)$ is the distribution of $\theta_n$ implied by
(\ref{eq:random}), (\ref{eq:mixture}), and (\ref{eq:partition}). The
population posterior predictive probability averages
(\ref{eq:population_probability}) over the posterior,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:posterior_predictive}
\Prob^{\mathrm{pop}}(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, y)
= \int \Prob(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \vartheta)\, p(\vartheta \mid y)\,
  \mathrm{d}\vartheta
\approx \frac{1}{D} \sum_{d = 1}^{D}
  \Prob\big(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \vartheta^{(d)}\big).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Population and conditional predictions} \label{sec:conditional}

For a decider $n$ in the sample, \code{type = "conditional"} replaces the
population distribution by the posterior draws of the decider's own coefficients
and class allocation (Section~\ref{sec:individual}),
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditional_predictive}
\Prob^{\mathrm{cond}}(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, y)
\approx \frac{1}{D} \sum_{d = 1}^{D}
  \Prob\big(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, \theta_n^{(d)},
  \tilde{\Sigma}^{(d)}\big),
\end{equation}
%
and thus exploits the choice history of the decider.

We compare both types of prediction for a repeated binary decision in an online
chess tournament. In arena tournaments on the chess server Lichess, a player may
go ``Berserk'' at the start of every game: the player's clock is halved, and a

the players of the Lichess Yearly Rapid Arena of April 2026, together with the

\subsection{Residuals} \label{sec:residuals}

The \fct{residuals} method returns the observed choice indicators minus the
posterior predictive probabilities of (\ref{eq:posterior_predictive}),
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residuals}
e_{ntj} = 1(y_{nt} = j)
  - \Prob^{\mathrm{pop}}(y_{nt} = j \mid X_{nt}, y),
\qquad j = 1, \dots, J,
\end{equation}
%
as a matrix with one row per choice occasion and one column per alternative. The
residuals of an occasion sum to zero, and with two alternatives, they differ
only in sign. A small residual of the chosen alternative indicates an accurately
predicted choice, whereas an absolute value close to one indicates a choice that
the model deems unlikely. The row names of the residual matrix combine the
decider and the choice occasion. For the first three choices of the first
traveler in \code{model\_train}, the residuals are:
%
\begin{Schunk}
\begin{Sinput}

R> head(train_residuals, 3)
\end{Sinput}
\begin{Soutput}

\end{Soutput}
\end{Schunk}

\section{Model evaluation} \label{sec:evaluation}

For the comparison of competing specifications, \pkg{RprobitB} provides three
criteria: the widely applicable information criterion (WAIC) of
\citet{Watanabe2010}, leave-one-out cross-validation approximated by
Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS-LOO) of \citet{Vehtari2017,
Vehtari2024}, and Bayes factors \citep{Kass1995}. The first two estimate the
expected predictive accuracy of a model for new deciders, whereas the third
compares the marginal likelihoods of two models. We write $p_n(\vartheta) =
p(y_n \mid X_n, \vartheta)$ for the likelihood contribution of decider $n$.
Since WAIC and PSIS-LOO evaluate $p_n$ under every retained draw, their cost
grows with the number of draws and deciders. Thinning or a parallel \pkg{future}
plan can be used to reduce the computational load.

We compare the three train models in \code{models}
(Section~\ref{sec:mixing_distributions}), whose price coefficient is fixed in
\code{model\_train}, normal in \code{price\_normal}, and negative log-normal in
\code{price\_lognormal}.

\subsection{Log-likelihood} \label{sec:loglik}

\fct{logLik} evaluates the log-likelihood $\sum_{n} \log p_n(\bar{\vartheta})$
at the posterior means $\bar{\vartheta}$ of the population parameters and
returns a \class{logLik} object, which \fct{AIC} and \fct{BIC} accept:
%
\begin{Schunk}
\begin{Sinput}
R> sapply(models, logLik)
\end{Sinput}
\begin{Soutput}
    model_train    price_normal price_lognormal 
      -1698.796       -1539.986       -1545.775 
\end{Soutput}
\end{Schunk}
%
The random price coefficient, which adds one free parameter to the five of
\code{model\_train}, raises the log-likelihood by 159 under the normal and by
153 under the negative log-normal mixing distribution. Since \fct{logLik},
\fct{AIC}, and \fct{BIC} rely on a single point estimate, they ignore the
posterior uncertainty about $\vartheta$, unlike WAIC and PSIS-LOO.

\subsection{WAIC and PSIS-LOO} \label{sec:loo}

Both criteria estimate the expected log predictive density (elpd) of a model for
the responses $\tilde{y}_n$ of new deciders,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elpd}
\mathrm{elpd} = \sum_{n = 1}^{N} \mathbb{E}\big[\log p(\tilde{y}_n \mid X_n,
y)\big], \qquad
p(\tilde{y}_n \mid X_n, y) = \int p(\tilde{y}_n \mid X_n, \vartheta)\,
p(\vartheta \mid y)\, \mathrm{d}\vartheta,
\end{equation}
%
which increases with the predictive accuracy. WAIC estimates the elpd as
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:waic}
\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{waic}} = \sum_{n = 1}^{N} \log \bigg(
\frac{1}{D} \sum_{d = 1}^{D} p_n\big(\vartheta^{(d)}\big) \bigg) - \sum_{n =
1}^{N} \mathrm{Var}_d\big[\log p_n(\vartheta^{(d)})\big], \qquad
\mathrm{WAIC} = -2\, \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{waic}},
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathrm{Var}_d$ is the sample variance over the $D$ draws. PSIS-LOO
instead estimates the leave-one-out predictive probability $p(y_n \mid y_{-n})$
of the responses of decider $n$ given the responses $y_{-n}$ of all other
deciders by importance sampling over the retained draws,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:psis_loo}
\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{loo}} = \sum_{n = 1}^{N} \log \hat{p}(y_n \mid
y_{-n}), \qquad
\hat{p}(y_n \mid y_{-n}) = \frac{\sum_{d = 1}^{D} w_n^{(d)}\,
p_n(\vartheta^{(d)})}{\sum_{d = 1}^{D} w_n^{(d)}},
\end{equation}
%
where the weights $w_n^{(d)}$ are Pareto-smoothed importance ratios. Both
estimates are sums of pointwise contributions $\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_n$. The
standard error of the difference between two models $\mathcal{M}_1$ and
$\mathcal{M}_2$ follows from the variance of the pointwise differences,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:elpd_difference}
\Delta_n = \widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{n}(\mathcal{M}_1) -
\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{n}(\mathcal{M}_2),
\qquad
\mathrm{se}\Big(\sum_{n = 1}^{N} \Delta_n\Big) = \sqrt{N\,
\mathrm{Var}_n(\Delta_n)},
\end{equation}
%
with $\mathrm{Var}_n$ the sample variance over the $N$ deciders
\citep{Vehtari2017}. \fct{WAIC} and \fct{loo} pass the $D \times N$ matrix of
the decider log-likelihoods to the \pkg{loo} package and return the objects of
that package. For \code{model_train}, \fct{WAIC} gives
$\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{waic}} = -1707.0$ with a standard error of

above which \citet{Vehtari2017} consider the WAIC approximation unreliable.
PSIS-LOO is then preferable, because it is more robust in such cases.

\subsection{Bayes factors} \label{sec:bayes_factors}

A Bayes factor compares two models $\mathcal{M}_1$ and $\mathcal{M}_2$ by the
ratio of their marginal likelihoods,
%
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bayes_factor}
\mathrm{BF}_{12} = \frac{p(y \mid \mathcal{M}_1)}{p(y \mid \mathcal{M}_2)},
\qquad
p(y \mid \mathcal{M}) = \int \prod_{n = 1}^{N} p(y_n \mid X_n, \vartheta,
\mathcal{M})\, p(\vartheta \mid \mathcal{M})\, \mathrm{d}\vartheta,
\end{equation}
%
where $p(\vartheta \mid \mathcal{M})$ is the prior of
Section~\ref{sec:est_prior}. \citet{Kass1995} interpret values of
$2 \log \mathrm{BF}_{12}$ above $10$ as very strong evidence for
$\mathcal{M}_1$. \fct{bayes\_factor} estimates the marginal likelihoods by
bridge sampling \citep{Meng1996, FruehwirthSchnatter2004, Gronau2017}.
%
\begin{Schunk}
\begin{Sinput}
R> log_bf <- bayes_factor(price_normal, model_train, log = TRUE)
R> log_bf
\end{Sinput}
\begin{Soutput}
Estimated log Bayes factor in favor of model1 over model2: 156.75518
\end{Soutput}
\end{Schunk}
%
The log Bayes factor of 157, that is, $2 \log
\mathrm{BF}_{12} \approx 314$, is very strong evidence
for the normal mixing distribution over the fixed price coefficient, in line
with the comparison by PSIS-LOO.

\section{Summary} \label{sec:summary}

The \proglang{R} package \pkg{RprobitB} implements the Bayesian estimation of
probit models for binary, multinomial, ordered, and ranked responses. All models
are specified through the single function \fct{fit}, whose formula interface
distinguishes the three covariate types of discrete choice data. The error
covariance is estimated, which permits flexible substitution patterns, and
preference heterogeneity is modeled through random coefficients with normal or
log-normal mixing distributions, through latent classes, or through both. The
number of latent classes can be fixed in advance or inferred from the data. For
efficient computation, the Gibbs sampler is implemented in \proglang{C++} and
can run several chains in parallel.

Since the derived quantities are evaluated under all retained draws, the
posterior uncertainty propagates to willingness-to-pay measures, marginal
effects, predicted choice probabilities, and the coefficients of individual
deciders. Conditional predictions incorporate the choice history of a decider,
and the posterior distribution of the number of latent classes quantifies the
uncertainty about the number of segments. Together with the standard methods of
\proglang{R} and the model comparison, the package covers a full analysis
workflow.

The package is intended for applied researchers and practitioners in
transportation, marketing, health, and related fields who analyze discrete
choice data and who report willingness-to-pay measures, marginal effects, or
predictions together with their uncertainty. Suggestions are welcome as issues
at \url{https://github.com/loelschlaeger/RprobitB/issues}.
